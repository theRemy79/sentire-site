**Acclaim remains an investigational cochlear implant in the United States as of November 6, 2026.** In its November 5 update, Envoy Medical reported that all 56 participants in its pivotal trial had passed six-month follow-up. The company said the study’s main effectiveness assessment uses 12-month data, so this milestone is progress in an ongoing study, rather than a final result. [Envoy’s November 5 update](https://ir.envoymedical.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/107/envoy-medical-reports-third-quarter-2026-results-and-highlights-continued-progress)

For people following hearing technology, a fully implanted option is an interesting development. But the news also raises a familiar question: what do the reported hearing scores actually tell us, and how do they relate to the audiograms we keep from our own appointments?

Here is what the latest announcements say, followed by a practical way to keep those different kinds of results in context.

## What changed in the latest Acclaim news?

Three dated announcements help explain where the project stands:

- **July 14, 2026:** Envoy released preliminary 12-month results for the first 10 participants in Stage 1. These were an early group’s results, rather than outcomes for the entire trial. [July data announcement](https://www.envoymedical.com/news/envoy-medical-reports-positive-12-month-data-showing-continued-improvement-in-speech-perception-in-study-of-first-of-its-kind-fully-implanted-cochlear-implant)
- **October 12, 2026:** The company announced submission of the second of four planned modules in its modular premarket approval application, or PMA. It described that module as primarily engineering information, with final clinical data to come in a later module. A submitted module is one part of an application, not permission to sell the device. [October regulatory update](https://www.envoymedical.com/news/envoy-medical-submits-second-module-of-modular-premarket-approval-application-to-fda-for-breakthrough-designated-acclaimr-cochlear-implant)
- **November 5, 2026:** Envoy confirmed the six-month milestone for all 56 participants. It expected the remaining 12-month visits to finish early in the second quarter of 2027, with the final clinical module submission to follow. These are company expectations, not an FDA decision or a confirmed launch date. [Latest company update](https://ir.envoymedical.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/107/envoy-medical-reports-third-quarter-2026-results-and-highlights-continued-progress)

## What does “fully implanted” mean here?

The usual cochlear implant arrangement includes both external and surgically implanted components. The [NIDCD cochlear implant overview](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/cochlear-implants) explains how the parts work together and how an implant differs from a hearing aid.

Envoy describes Acclaim as using an implanted sensor that picks up vibrations through the ear’s anatomy, with processing and a rechargeable battery also inside the body. Its trial information says external accessories control and charge the system, including a charging coil placed over the battery in the chest. Those accessories are not required to hear. **Fully implanted does not mean charging-free or accessory-free.** [Envoy’s trial device and charger explanation](https://www.envoymedical.com/acclaim-pivotal)

That design is worth understanding on its own terms. It does not establish that Acclaim will produce better results than another implant for a particular person.

## Is Acclaim FDA approved, and when could it be available?

Envoy’s [Acclaim product page](https://www.envoymedical.com/acclaim-cochlear-implant) still identifies it as investigational and limited to investigational use in the United States. It is not presented as an FDA-approved option for general U.S. sale in the material reviewed for this article.

You may also see its **Breakthrough Device designation** mentioned. The [FDA explains that this program helps speed development and review while preserving safety and effectiveness standards](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/how-study-and-market-your-device/breakthrough-devices-program). Designation and marketing authorization are separate steps; the designation does not guarantee approval.

There is no confirmed U.S. release date in the updates reviewed here. Keep the dates for trial follow-up, application submission and any future FDA decision separate when reading headlines.

## What does the reported word-recognition score mean?

Envoy’s July announcement reported a mean **CNC word-recognition score of 53.2% at 12 months**, compared with **15.2% before implantation**, for all 10 Stage 1 participants. That is a **38-percentage-point difference**. CNC stands for consonant–nucleus–consonant. The company also identified one participant as a potential non-responder. [Original results and limitations](https://www.envoymedical.com/news/envoy-medical-reports-positive-12-month-data-showing-continued-improvement-in-speech-perception-in-study-of-first-of-its-kind-fully-implanted-cochlear-implant)

Those numbers summarize performance on a particular word-recognition test in a small group. They do not mean “53.2% of normal hearing,” describe every participant’s outcome, or predict your own result. They also are not a head-to-head comparison with another cochlear implant.

These are manufacturer-reported, preliminary results from an ongoing trial. They should not be treated as the completed study’s findings or as a personal treatment recommendation.

## Where your audiograms fit into the story

An audiogram and a speech-test percentage describe different measurements. The [ASHA-linked guide to hearing tests](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/types-of-hearing-tests) explains that pure-tone testing records the softest tones heard at different pitches, while speech testing assesses hearing and understanding spoken words or sentences.

| Result on a report | What it records |
| --- | --- |
| Pure-tone thresholds on an audiogram | Hearing levels at particular frequencies, commonly shown in dB HL. |
| Word- or sentence-recognition results | Performance on the named speech test, under its recorded test conditions. |

A speech score is not a point to plot on the audiogram’s dB scale. Keep both results, with their original labels, rather than reducing a whole appointment to one number.

For speech results, keep the **test name, date, ear or ears tested, presentation level, and whether hearing aids or an implant were used**, when the report supplies those details. Keep any information about quiet versus background noise too. ASHA’s [adult hearing assessment guidance](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/hearing-loss/) describes these different assessment components and the importance of test conditions.

If a detail is missing, leave it unknown and ask the clinic. Two percentages from differently labeled tests should prompt a question about comparability, rather than an automatic conclusion that hearing improved or worsened.

## Turn the news into useful appointment questions

Keep a complete copy of each audiogram and any separate speech-test pages. Then make a short question list:

- Which of my results are hearing thresholds, and which are speech-understanding scores?
- Which speech tests were performed with hearing aids or an implant, and under what conditions?
- Are my older and newer results directly comparable?
- What options are relevant to my hearing and circumstances now, and what follow-up would you recommend?

Your clinical team can explain what your own results mean and assess your options. A company’s trial average, an audiogram viewed alone, or a record-keeping app cannot make that assessment for you.
