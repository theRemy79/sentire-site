To track your hearing test results over time, keep a copy of every report, organize them by test date, and record enough detail to identify what was measured. Keep the originals alongside any spreadsheet or app you use, and bring questions about changes to your audiologist.

You can start with a folder on your computer. The useful part is having a routine you can repeat after each appointment, so finding an older result takes a moment rather than a search through several portals or email threads.

## 1. Keep a complete copy of each hearing test

After an appointment, ask your clinic how to obtain your audiogram and any accompanying results. If a patient portal offers a downloadable PDF, save a copy somewhere you control. For a paper report, make a clear scan or photograph of every page, including the date, labels, and symbol legend.

An audiogram charts hearing thresholds across different pitches. The [ASHA-linked audiogram explainer](https://helpingyoucommunicate.org/article/audiograms-and-hearing-loss-configuration) describes the graph and why the individual results matter. Your report may also contain speech-test results or other information outside that graph, so keep the complete document.

Before filing a copy, check that it opens and that small labels are readable. A cropped graph without its date or legend can be difficult to make sense of later.

## 2. Give your hearing history a simple structure

Choose one main folder, such as **Hearing records**, and name files with the test date first. Use a consistent pattern:

`2026-04-16_example-clinic_audiogram.pdf`

The year-month-day order keeps filenames in date order when sorted by name. Use the date the test happened, which may differ from the date you downloaded the file. If you have several documents from the same visit, add a short description to distinguish them.

Keep a small index in a document, spreadsheet, or hearing history tracker. Useful fields include:

- **Test date and clinic:** enough to find the original report again.
- **Source filename:** the location of the complete PDF or scan.
- **Test details:** information the report supplies, such as which ear was tested and whether testing was aided or unaided.
- **Notes and questions:** your own observations, clearly separated from the clinic’s findings.

You do not need to transcribe every number immediately. Start by making your existing records easy to find. If a detail is missing, leave it unknown rather than guessing.

## 3. Compare audiograms with the labels intact

When you compare audiograms over time, start with two dated reports. Check which symbols belong to each ear and which measurements they represent. A report may contain air-conduction and bone-conduction results, masking symbols, or marks indicating no response. Preserve those distinctions when copying values.

If the layouts differ, ask your audiologist to help identify corresponding measurements. Avoid treating every mark at the same pitch as interchangeable, or filling an untested frequency from a line drawn between points.

One practical approach is to compare a recent test with an earlier reference test, then look through the intervening visits. Keep the underlying values and source reports available, even if a summary chart makes the history easier to browse.

The aim is to prepare clear questions: “What changed between these tests?” or “Are these results directly comparable?” Your audiologist can explain the significance of a difference in the context of your assessment. A record-keeping tool cannot determine its cause or what treatment you need.

## 4. Keep everyday observations separate from measurements

A test date is a useful place to attach a short note about what you wanted to discuss at that visit. You might record difficulty following a particular conversation, a question about your hearing aids, or something your clinician asked you to watch for.

Label these as personal observations. A note about how hearing felt on a particular day is different from a measured hearing threshold. Keeping both clearly identified helps you explain your experience without turning an observation into a test result.

Keep notes brief enough to revisit. A few dated sentences and a specific question can be more useful at an appointment than pages you have to search through.

I built Sentire because I wanted an easier way to follow my own hearing history. The [story behind Sentire](/blog/why-i-built-sentire/) explains that starting point. Here is a simple workflow for using it with existing reports:

## 6. Keep originals and backups you can recover

Whichever method you choose, keep an independent backup. A folder or app database on one computer can be lost with that device.

In Sentire, a JSON export or SQLite backup can restore saved hearing-history data. A PDF is a readable report for sharing. Keep your original imported reports and license email separately: they are not included in the hearing-history backup. The [backup guide](/guide/#backup-restore) explains restoration and its replacement behavior.

Hearing records contain personal information. Protect your device and backup location, and share files deliberately. ## A checklist for your next appointment

Before your next visit, gather:

- Your latest report and an earlier report you want to discuss.
- The test dates and clinic details for each.
- Any relevant notes, labeled as your observations.
- Two or three questions about the results or your everyday hearing.
- A readable copy you can bring or share through your clinic’s preferred method.

Keeping hearing test results organized gives you a record to return to and a clearer starting point for that conversation. You can begin today with the reports you already have.
